▸ LEGENDARY MORSE CODES ◂

You don't need to know radio to feel a chill down your spine over certain Morse signals. Here's a collection of codes and prosigns that were actually used at sea, in the sky, and across more than a century of real radio history.

1844
Where it all began

"What hath God wrought"— Samuel Morse, May 24, 1844, the first-ever telegraph message

Sent from Washington, D.C. to Baltimore, this was the first message ever transmitted by electrical telegraph. Quoting a line from the Bible, Morse's invention — turning letters into electrical pulses and sending them across distance — took the first step toward a communication network that would one day connect the entire world.

CQD
The distress call that came before SOS

Before SOS was adopted, Marconi stations used this as their distress signal. In 1912, Titanic's wireless operator Jack Phillips kept sending both CQD and SOS right up to the moment the ship went down. SOS was still new at the time — his colleague Harold Bride is said to have joked, "Send SOS; it's your last chance to send it."

SOS
The universal distress signal

The most famous Morse code of all. Officially adopted as the international distress signal in 1908. It isn't short for "Save Our Ship" or "Save Our Souls" — it was simply chosen because three dots, three dashes, three dots is easy to send and unmistakable to hear. That simplicity alone has saved countless lives.

V
Beethoven's "Fate," and the symbol of victory

Three dots and a dash — the same rhythm that opens Beethoven's Symphony No. 5, "Fate." During World War II, the BBC broadcast this "V" across Nazi-occupied Europe as a signal for "Victory." Tapped out on church bells and whistled on street corners, it eventually fed into Churchill's own "V for Victory" hand sign.

CQ
"Is anybody out there?"

A general call to no one in particular — "calling any station." From the telegraph era to today's amateur radio, it's still the first word sent when reaching out to a stranger somewhere out there. The simplest possible call into the open sky.

73 / 88
Telegraph-era slang that never went away

73 means "best regards," 88 means "love and kisses" — numeric shorthand born in the days of wired telegraphy. These abbreviations are still exchanged as a sign-off by amateur radio operators all over the world today.

VOR / NDB
Still guiding pilots through the sky, even in the GPS era

Aviation navigation beacons (VOR and NDB stations) continuously transmit their own 2-3 letter identifier in Morse code to this day. Pilots listen for this faint tone through their headset to confirm they're tuned to the correct beacon — not just trusting the instrument readout. Even in an age of GPS, Morse is still quietly on duty keeping the skies safe.

DE
The prosign that means "from"

"DE" is the prosign for "from," sent right before your own callsign after a CQ call. "CQ CQ DE JL1GIX" means "Calling any station, this is JL1GIX." Every contact with a stranger starts with these two small letters.

R
The secret radio origin of "Roger"

The word "Roger," familiar from movies and radio chatter, was originally just the phonetic alphabet word for the letter "R." In Morse communication "R" meant "received" — when voice radio took over, operators kept saying "Roger" to mean the same thing, and it's been radio slang for "understood" ever since.

QSL
A postcard that proves you were really heard

"QSL" is shorthand (a Q-code) for "I confirm receipt." In amateur radio culture, operators who've made contact exchange postcard-like "QSL cards" as a keepsake proof of the conversation. It's a small, lasting tradition that turns an invisible radio wave into something you can hold in your hand.

K・AR・SK
The three signals that opened, closed, and finally silenced an era

"K" means "go ahead, over." "AR" means "end of message." "SK" means "end of contact" — all prosigns, special codes formed by running two letters together without a gap. On January 31, 1997, as French coastal stations bid farewell to Morse-based distress watch, one of them sent this:

"CALLING all. This is our last cry before our eternal silence."— a French coastal radio station, January 31, 1997

Then, at 0000 UTC on February 1, 1999, the world's maritime communication officially shifted from Morse code to the satellite- and radio-based GMDSS. The code that had watched over ships for more than a century quietly stood down.
